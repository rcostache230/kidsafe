The first time my daughter asked to download an app I knew was risky, I could have said no and ended the conversation. Instead, I asked why she wanted it.

Her three closest friends were using it to plan a school project. She was scared of being left out of the group chat. I was thinking about security; she was thinking about her friends.

## What the settings can't do

The guides on this site help you set up a device. Your child also needs to trust you enough to ask for help when something goes wrong.

Strict controls alone can encourage children to wait until they're outside the house to try something. Conversations alone leave gaps too: a child can encounter content they aren't ready for. I use both controls and conversation.

## Three questions I ask

1. **What are you using it for?** Ask what they want to do in the app.
2. **What do your friends do on it?** Find out how the group uses it.
3. **What would you tell me first if something weird happened?** Practice that conversation before it's needed.

Answering these together makes the controls part of an agreement the child understands.

## Keep talking as they grow

I want my daughter to understand why a filter is there. As children get older, they may find ways around screen-time limits. At 16, the relationship matters as much as the settings: will they still come to you when something goes wrong?

When you change a setting, explain it. Leave room for your child to ask questions.
