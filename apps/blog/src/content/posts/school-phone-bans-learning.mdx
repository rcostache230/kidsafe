Keeping phones out of reach during the school day improves students' test scores, according to a large new US study. Schools that required pupils to store their phones in lockable pouches saw faster progress in maths and reading, equivalent to about one or two extra weeks of learning each year. [Harvard's study announcement][release]

Reporting on the findings on 17 November, [The New York Times described][nyt] evidence that school phone bans are gradually improving scores after earlier research produced mixed results. The improvement is modest in any one year, but a sustained gain could accumulate over several years. The learning-time comparison describes progress on tests; the schools did not add extra days to the calendar.

## What did the researchers measure?

The team at Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research, Dartmouth and NWEA analysed results from approximately 3.3 million students across 4,600 US schools between the 2021–22 and 2025–26 school years. They used MAP Growth assessments in grades 3–10 to measure progress from autumn to spring. [Harvard's study announcement][release]

For the pouch analysis, the researchers compared schools adopting lockable phone pouches with schools matched on characteristics such as prior achievement and pupil demographics. They examined whether learning growth changed after adoption, accounting for students' starting scores. Schools were not randomly assigned to the policy, so the comparison cannot rule out every other change at those schools. [Full research report, sections IV–V][report]

## How much difference did putting phones away make?

The estimated improvement in annual growth was about 0.01 standard deviations in both subjects, a small average effect. The researchers translate this into the one-to-two-week learning equivalent. Gains appeared to persist beyond the first year, though there is less evidence about schools with several years of implementation. [Findings and summary][release]

Small annual improvements matter when they continue over time. For schools, the finding supports keeping phones out of reach during the day. For an individual family, it describes an average benefit rather than a prediction of how much one child's marks will change.

## A strict ban means more than a rule on paper

The main finding concerns schools using lockable pouches throughout the school day. It does not establish that asking students to silence a phone in their pocket will produce the same result. Nor does it show that buying a particular product is necessary: the achievement analysis focused on one approach. [Study overview][overview]

The report also examined statewide bans introduced in 2025–26, with mixed findings. States adopting those policies had different learning trends beforehand, making those estimates harder to interpret as effects of the bans themselves. [Research report][report]

The study examines US test-score growth. Applying its estimates to a Romanian school requires caution, and it does not settle questions about wellbeing, friendships or phone use at home.

## What should parents ask their school?

The practical questions go beyond whether phones are allowed. Before a new policy starts, ask:

- **Where will phones go?** Find out who stores them, who can unlock them, and what happens if a device is lost or damaged.
- **How will families make contact?** Agree on a reliable route through the school for urgent messages and changes to collection arrangements.
- **How will individual needs be handled?** Ask how the school will arrange access for pupils who use a device for a medical or accessibility need.
- **How will the school review the policy?** Ask staff to look at classroom interruptions, pupils' experience and learning over time, alongside how fairly the rules are applied.

These are questions for planning a workable policy, rather than findings tested by this study.

Talk with your child about the school day too. When would they most want to check their phone? What would make putting it away easier? If your family wants to try a similar routine during homework, agree on a place to leave devices and a time to check messages. Our guide to [screen time without a fight](/blog/en/screen-time-without-a-fight) can help start that conversation.

This study found that strict school phone bans improved learning growth. Putting that finding into practice means arranging reliable family contact, supporting individual needs and listening to pupils and teachers as the policy takes effect.
